Problem Definition

Problem Statement

People using instant payment systems like UPI are being tricked into sending money to fraudsters themselves. Current safety checks confirm who is paying (PIN, OTP, device) but not whether the payment makes sense, and most help comes only after the money is gone. There is no common tool that, at the moment of payment, estimates how risky that particular payment is, explains the reason to the payer, and slows down only the risky ones without bothering everyone else.

A few things stand out. Losses roughly tripled from 2023 to 2024. There are more than ten lakh UPI fraud cases a year, which is far too many to handle by hand. RBI's data for FY2024-25 shows card and internet frauds were about two-thirds of bank fraud cases but only about 7 % of the amount rbi_trend, which means a very large number of small losses to ordinary people, the kind that rarely get investigated in depth. And about half the victims in the LocalCircles survey never complained at all localcircles_nc, so the real numbers are higher.

The common patterns are well known by now. In a fake KYC call, the victim is told their account will be blocked unless they pay a small "verification" amount. In a "digital arrest", a caller posing as police or customs keeps the victim on a video call for hours and demands a deposit. Job and task scams ask for a registration fee and then bigger "deposits", and the receiving account is a mule collecting from dozens of people a day. In a remote-access scam the victim installs a screen-sharing app "for support" and several quick payments of similar amounts follow. Account takeover uses stolen credentials from a new phone, often at night, to send most of the balance to someone new.

Root Causes

We think the problem comes from five things working together.

Psychology. Scams use authority, urgency and fear stajano2011. A person who has just been told they will be arrested is not going to calmly check the payee's details.

Authentication is not intent. The UPI PIN proves the account holder is present. It says nothing about whether they understand where the money is going.

The payer has less information than the platform. The system knows that an account is two days old or has been reported three times. The payer only sees a name.

Payments are instant and final. Money is passed through mule accounts within minutes, so recovery after the fact is hard.

Warnings are generic. Messages like "never share your OTP" are shown to everyone all the time, and studies show people learn to ignore warnings they see often akhawe2013, vance2018.

It has not been solved yet for practical reasons. Fraud data sits inside each bank and is sensitive, so it is hard to share or research. False alarms are expensive at UPI's volume: UPI averaged about 66 crore payments a day in FY2025-26, so even a 1 % false alarm rate would interrupt around 66 lakh genuine payments every day. Scoring has to finish in milliseconds. Black-box scores are hard to justify to customers, while rule-only systems miss new patterns. And scammers keep changing their scripts, so models trained on old data slowly go stale dalpozzolo2018.

Who Is Affected

Senior citizens are the main target of "bank", "pension" and "digital arrest" calls and often lose savings. Students and young earners get pulled into job and task scams, usually in small repeated payments, and many are too embarrassed to report it. Small shopkeepers face fake refund and QR code tricks, and their normal pattern of receiving money from many people can look like a mule account to a careless system. Banks and payment apps carry the support load and the damage to their reputation, and in some countries they now have to refund victims uk_psr. The police deal with huge complaint volumes across states. Over time, all of this makes people trust digital payments less.
