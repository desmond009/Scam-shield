Problem Definition

Problem Statement

Problem statement. Users of instant, irrevocable payment systems such as UPI are being manipulated into authorising payments to fraudsters. Existing safeguards verify who is paying (PIN, OTP, device) but not whether the payment makes sense, and they act mostly after the money has gone. There is no widely available mechanism that, at the moment of payment, (a) estimates how likely this specific payment is to be a scam, (b) explains why in plain language, and (c) adds friction in proportion to the risk — without slowing down the overwhelming majority of genuine payments.

The problem has three parts. The detection problem is to estimate scam risk from behavioural signals within a few hundred milliseconds, with extremely imbalanced data (scams are a tiny fraction of payments) and adversaries who adapt. The intervention problem is to change the victim's behaviour at the critical moment, even when a scammer is coaching them on the phone, without training all users to ignore warnings. The feedback problem is to turn every confirmed scam into protection for the next potential victim — for example by blocking a mule account for all users once an analyst confirms it.

Five observations follow from these numbers. First, the absolute loss is large and growing: reported cyber financial-fraud losses roughly tripled between 2023 and 2024. Second, the volume of UPI fraud cases (more than a million a year) is too large for manual handling; any defence must be automated. Third, the number of UPI fraud cases fell slightly between FY24 and FY25 while UPI volume kept growing, which suggests that controls do work — the problem is tractable. Fourth, this is high-volume, low-value retail fraud: in RBI's data for FY2024-25, card and internet frauds made up about two-thirds of bank fraud cases but only about 7 % of the amount involved rbi_trend — many small losses to ordinary people, which are rarely worth a long investigation and therefore have to be prevented rather than pursued. Fifth, the true extent is under-reported: about half of victims surveyed never file a complaint localcircles_nc.

Behind the statistics are typical stories, several of which we heard from people we know:

  • Fake KYC / bank call. “Your account will be blocked today unless you complete KYC.” The victim pays a “verification fee” or is guided to a “refund” that is actually a payment.
  • Digital arrest. A caller posing as police or customs claims a parcel in the victim's name contained drugs and demands a “security deposit” to avoid arrest, keeping the victim on a video call for hours.
  • Job and task scams. A “part-time job” requires a small registration fee, then larger “deposits” to unlock earnings. The collecting account is a mule paid by dozens of victims in a day.
  • Remote-access drain. The victim is persuaded to install a screen-sharing app “for support”; the fraudster then makes several rapid payments of similar amounts.
  • Account takeover. Stolen credentials are used from a new device, often at night, after several failed attempts, to send most of the balance to a new payee.

Root Causes

Why the problem exists

  1. Social engineering exploits predictable psychology. Scams rely on authority, urgency, fear, reciprocity and distraction stajano2011. A frightened person on a call does not stop to check a payee's details.
  2. Authentication is not intent verification. A UPI PIN proves that the account holder is present; it says nothing about whether they understand where the money is going. In APP fraud the right person enters the right PIN.
  3. The payer lacks information the system has. The platform knows that a payee account is two days old, has been reported three times or received money from 15 strangers today. The payer sees only a name.
  4. Irreversibility and speed. Settlement is instant, and funds are layered through mule accounts within minutes, so post-transaction recovery is hard.
  5. Warnings are generic and frequent. Blanket messages (“never share your OTP”) are shown to everyone, all the time, and people habituate to them akhawe2013, vance2018.

Why it has not been solved yet

  • Data is siloed and sensitive. Fraud labels sit inside individual banks and PSPs; sharing them is constrained by privacy law and competition. Research on real payment fraud data is therefore scarce outside large institutions.
  • False positives are expensive. With 800 million payments a day, even a 1 % false-alarm rate would interrupt millions of genuine payments. Banks therefore tune systems conservatively, and many risky payments pass.
  • Latency constraints. Real-time payment rails leave very little time for scoring; complex models must answer in milliseconds.
  • Explainability. Black-box scores are hard to justify to customers, regulators and analysts; rule-only systems are explainable but miss novel patterns.
  • Adversaries adapt. Scam scripts change monthly. Static rules decay, and models trained on last year's data drift dalpozzolo2018.
