User Research

User research had two purposes: to confirm that the problem matters to real people, and to make design decisions — which signals to use, how much friction is acceptable, how warnings should be worded — on the basis of evidence rather than our own assumptions. This chapter presents the personas we designed for, the questionnaire used to validate the problem, the evidence gathered, and the user journey.

Research Approach

We combined three sources of evidence:

  1. Secondary research — government data, regulator publications and large published surveys (Chapter 2), in particular the 2025 LocalCircles survey of more than 32,000 respondents across 365 districts localcircles.
  2. Primary research — a structured questionnaire (Appendix A) for UPI users, and short semi-structured conversations with family members, classmates and local shopkeepers about scam attempts they had faced.
  3. Literature on warning design — field studies showing that people habituate to frequent, generic security warnings and respond better to rare, specific and varied ones akhawe2013, anderson2015, vance2018.

The personas below are composite personas in the sense of Cooper cooper2004: each combines traits observed across several people and published reports, and none describes a real individual. The same four user types drive the synthetic data generator (Section 8.8), so the system is designed and evaluated for the same people.

User Personas

Persona 1 — Kamala Rao, 67, retired schoolteacher (Hubballi)
BackgroundLives with her husband; son works in Bengaluru and set up her UPI app. Receives a monthly pension. Makes about three payments a day (vegetables, medicines, electricity), typically ₹100–₹800, during the day. GoalsPay bills independently without bothering her son; keep her savings safe. Pain pointsTrusts callers who sound official; fears her account or pension will be “blocked”; small screen text; does not know what a “collect request” is. BehaviourFollows instructions step by step; panics under time pressure; rarely reads long messages. Scam riskFake KYC / bank / pension calls; “digital arrest”. What she needsA clear, calm “stop” at the right moment, one sentence that explains why, and a big, obvious safe option.
Persona 2 — Rohan Kulkarni, 20, engineering student (Dharwad)
BackgroundMonthly allowance from parents; about seven small payments a day (canteen, auto, recharges, ₹50–₹300), often late at night. GoalsFast, frictionless payments; earn some money part-time. Pain pointsAnnoyed by any delay; sees “work-from-home / task” offers on social media; embarrassed to report losses to parents. BehaviourTech-savvy but over-confident; ignores generic warnings; pays new people often (friends, hostel contacts). Scam riskJob/task scams paying a mule account; investment “groups”. What he needsNo friction for everyday payments, and a specific warning (“this account is collecting from many people today”) when it matters.
Persona 3 — Imran Shaikh, 38, kirana shop owner (Belagavi)
BackgroundReceives about 25 payments a day; pays suppliers in batches of three to five within minutes; amounts ₹500–₹20,000. GoalsKeep the queue moving; pay suppliers on time; avoid fake “I sent too much, please refund” tricks. Pain pointsCannot tolerate false alarms on routine supplier payments; often uses a second phone at the counter. BehaviourHigh volume, repetitive, time-pressured; legitimate patterns that look like velocity and new devices. Scam riskFake buyer refunds, QR-code swaps, account takeover. What he needsA system that learns his normal behaviour, so batches and festival rush do not trigger warnings.
Persona 4 — Priya Nair, 29, fraud-operations analyst at a mid-size bank (B2B user)
BackgroundReviews flagged transactions in a vendor FRM tool; part of a small team handling a large queue. GoalsReview the riskiest cases first; make defensible decisions quickly; stop mule accounts early. Pain pointsAlert fatigue; opaque scores without reasons; no way to see that ten victims paid the same new account today; rule changes need IT tickets. BehaviourWorks from a queue; wants keyboard-fast review, full context on one screen, and a feedback loop that visibly improves the system. What she needsLive alerts with score breakdown, reasons, model contributions and payee history; one-click “confirm fraud” that blocks the payee for everyone.

Survey and Interview Questionnaire

The questionnaire (reproduced in full in Appendix A) has 20 questions in five sections, designed to take under five minutes on a phone. Table 3.1 shows its structure and the hypothesis each section tests.

Structure of the validation questionnaire
SectionQuestionsHypothesis tested
A. Demographics & usageQ1–Q5Our personas cover the main user types; most respondents use UPI daily.
B. Exposure to scamsQ6–Q10H1: Most respondents (or their families) have received a scam attempt; scams are mainly social engineering, not hacking.
C. Losses and recoveryQ11–Q13H2: Victims rarely recover money and often do not report.

E. Trust and privacyQ19–Q20H5: Users accept behavioural analysis of their payments if the purpose is limited to fraud prevention.

Summary of Responses and Key Insights

Evidence from published surveys

The large published surveys strongly support H1 and H2. In the 2025 LocalCircles survey, about 20 % of UPI-using families reported at least one fraud incident since 2022; among victims, about half reported that their PIN or settings were compromised and about 40 % lost money after clicking a payment link localcircles. About 51 % of victims never filed a complaint localcircles_nc. Government data show “digital arrest” cases — a pure social-engineering scam — tripling from 39,925 in 2022 to 1,23,672 in 2024 digital_arrest.

Key insights that shaped the design

Research insights and the design decisions they led to
InsightDesign decision in ScamShield
Scams are mostly persuasion of a legitimate user; the PIN is entered by the real owner.Score the payment, not just the login; add context questions that capture what transaction data cannot (a caller applying pressure, a request for an OTP or app install).
People habituate to frequent, generic warnings akhawe2013, vance2018.No warning at all for LOW risk (the vast majority); warnings are specific (“this account is two days old”) and limited to the top three reasons.
Time pressure is the scammer's main tool stajano2011.HIGH risk imposes a ten-second pause and requires the UPI ID to be retyped; the safe action (“Cancel and report”) is the most prominent button.
A coached victim may lie to the app.Questionnaire answers can only raise the score, never lower it.
Merchants and festival shoppers have unusual but legitimate behaviour.Per-user baselines (30-day mean, spread, maximum, usual hour, known payees and devices); hard-negative scenarios in the training data.
Victims rarely report, and reports arrive late.One-tap “Cancel and report” at the moment of the warning; reports raise the payee's risk for every user.
Analysts need context, not just a score.Analyst view shows rule points, ML score and top contributing features, all reasons, and the payee's history; FRAUD verdict blocks the payee platform-wide.
