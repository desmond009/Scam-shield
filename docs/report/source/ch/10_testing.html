Model Evaluation Results

Evaluation protocol

The reference model (gbm-20261014-074524) was trained on 96,118 payments (fraud rate 2.48 %), calibrated on 22,734 (3.11 %), and evaluated on a time-based holdout of the last 25 % of the timeline: 45,387 payments, 779 scams (1.72 %), including a festival period never seen in training. Because the classes are highly imbalanced, we report precision, recall and false-positive rate at the operating thresholds and use PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC as the primary ranking measure saito2015. Three systems are compared: rules-only, ML-only and the hybrid blend.

The headline comparison: recall at matched false-positive rate

The definition of done required the hybrid to beat rules-only in recall at a comparable false-positive rate. At the FPR at which rules-only operates (3.71 %), the hybrid threshold that gives a comparable FPR (3.65 %, score ≥ 17.5) catches 95.6 % of scams (745 of 779), against 73.3 % (571 of 779) for rules alone — an absolute gain of 22.3 percentage points. The criterion is met.

Honest finding. ML-only ranks payments slightly better than the hybrid (PR-AUC 0.946 vs 0.933) and has higher recall at the MEDIUM threshold. The rule component costs a little ranking quality. In exchange it provides human-readable reasons, carries the hard overrides, keeps working when the ML service is down, and — at our operating thresholds — gives the hybrid the best precision and the lowest false-positive rate (0.43 % at MEDIUM; 0.056 % at HIGH). For a system that interrupts real people, we judge that trade-off worthwhile, and we show it openly on the Model page.

Figure 10.2 shows why the bands sit where they do: at 35, recall is still above 93 % while the false-positive rate has fallen below 0.5 %; at 70, precision reaches 96 %, which justifies the stronger HIGH intervention.

Results by scenario

The hybrid catches 91 % of impersonation, 99 % of mule-collection, 89 % of velocity-drain and 99 % of account-takeover payments. Rules alone are weakest on account takeover (57 %), where the combination of signals matters more than any single threshold. On the false-alarm side, the hybrid flags 0.04 % of everyday payments, 1.0 % of festival payments and none of the shopkeepers' supplier batches — which rules alone flag at 11.6 %. Its weakest areas are first payments to new contacts (21.7 % flagged, n = 60) and new merchants (31.3 %, n = 16): small groups, but exactly the legitimate behaviour that most resembles a scam, and a known limitation.

Calibration and feature importance

Isotonic calibration improved the Brier score from 0.0046 to 0.0035 on the holdout. The calibration curve shows that the model is over-confident in the 30–60 % range on the holdout: the holdout contains a festival period that shifts behaviour, and those bins contain few payments. We show this rather than hide it. The most important features are payee age, amount z-score, distinct senders to the payee in 24 hours and repeated similar amounts — signals that real fraud teams also use. Feature importances were inspected after every change to the generator; this is how two data leaks were discovered (Section 11.1).

Performance Results

Under a 60-second load of 20 requests per second over 10 connections, every request ran the full hybrid pipeline with zero errors and zero ML fallbacks. Client-measured latency was p50 42 ms, p90 87 ms, p99 118 ms, maximum 147 ms; the API's own metrics reported p95 109 ms — well within the 300 ms budget (NFR-1). The first load test exposed a bottleneck: scikit-learn's OpenMP thread pool made single-row predictions slow under concurrency (47 req/s, p50 205 ms), which tripped the circuit breaker. Setting OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 raised ML throughput to 154 req/s (p50 61 ms) and the breaker stayed closed. The tested rate of 20 req/s reflects the per-user rate limit and the 12 test accounts, not a measured maximum.

User Feedback and Acceptance Testing

User acceptance testing was planned around the four personas with the following script: (1) make three everyday payments; (2) pay a “KYC desk” while a team member plays a pressuring caller; (3) pay a known contact a large amount (rent); (4) as an analyst, review the alerts created. Acceptance criteria were: everyday payments complete without any prompt; the participant understands, in their own words, why a payment was flagged; the participant cancels at least the coached scam payment; and the participant rates the HIGH friction as acceptable.
