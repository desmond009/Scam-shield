Conclusion

We started with a simple observation. Most UPI fraud happens because people are convinced to send money themselves, and PINs and OTPs cannot stop that. Our answer was to check each payment in the short gap after the user presses Pay and before the money moves, and to tell the user what we see.

ScamShield does this with 20 behavioural features, 11 rules and a calibrated gradient boosting model. On a synthetic test period of 45,387 payments that the model never saw, the hybrid caught 95.6 % of scams at the false alarm rate where rules alone caught 73.3 %. At our chosen thresholds it also had the best precision of the three systems we compared. ML alone ranked payments slightly better, and we found and fixed two leaks in our own data that had made the first results look almost perfect. We have reported both of these, because we think they matter as much as the headline number.

The rest of the system is built around how people actually behave. Most payments see no warning at all. Risky ones get a short, specific explanation and three questions, and the riskiest get a pause and a re-typed UPI ID, with "Cancel and report" as the easiest choice. When analysts confirm a scam account, it is blocked for everyone. The system met its performance target (p95 around 110 ms against 300 ms), keeps working when the ML service fails, and passes 341 automated tests.

The biggest open question is how well it works on real data. Our numbers come from a simulation, and the next step is a shadow-mode pilot with a real partner. Still, we think the project shows that a payment app can do more than ask for a PIN. It can notice when something is off, explain it in plain words, and give the user a moment to think before the money is gone.
