Conclusion

This project set out to address a problem that the authentication layer of digital payments cannot solve on its own: people being persuaded to authorise payments to scammers. Our answer, ScamShield, moves the defence to the one moment when it can still prevent the loss — after the user presses Pay and before the money moves — and treats that moment as both a detection problem and a human-design problem.

On the detection side, we built a hybrid engine that combines eleven transparent, runtime-configurable rules with a calibrated gradient-boosting model over twenty behavioural features, computed identically in TypeScript and Python. Evaluated on a time-based synthetic holdout of 45,387 payments that the model never saw, the hybrid catches 95.6 % of scams at the false-positive rate at which rules alone catch 73.3 %, and at the operating thresholds it achieves the best precision (79.1 % at MEDIUM, 96.0 % at HIGH) and the lowest false-positive rate (0.43 % and 0.056 %) of the three systems compared. We also report what did not favour us: the ML model alone ranks payments slightly better than the hybrid, the model is over-confident in part of the probability range during the festival period, and the first version of our data contained two leaks that made results look near-perfect until we found and fixed them.

On the human side, the system intervenes in proportion to risk: it stays silent for the great majority of payments, explains a medium-risk payment in plain language and asks three questions that capture the context transaction data cannot see, and imposes deliberate friction — a pause and re-typing of the recipient's UPI ID — on high-risk payments, with “Cancel and report” as the obvious choice. Analysts receive live alerts with full explanations, and their verdicts protect every other user by blocking confirmed scam accounts and feeding retraining.

On the engineering side, the system meets its non-functional requirements: p95 latency of about 110 ms against a 300 ms budget, graceful degradation to rules-only scoring when the model is unavailable, idempotent and double-spend-safe payments, and 341 passing automated tests.

The most important limitation is also the clearest next step: every result in this report comes from synthetic data. The method — leak-free features, time-based splits, calibration, fair comparison against a baseline, and honest reporting — is what we are confident in; the numbers themselves must be re-established on real data in a shadow-mode pilot before the system is trusted with real payments. The product analysis suggests that such a pilot is both feasible and worthwhile: India's fraud-prevention market is growing at about 20 % a year, regulation now expects risk-based checks on digital payments, and smaller banks and payment apps lack an explainable, payer-facing layer of protection.

ScamShield lowers risk; it does not and cannot guarantee that every scam will be caught. But by giving people the right information, at the right moment, with the right amount of friction, it shows how a payment system can become an active ally of its users against those who would deceive them.
